How compound interest builds wealth

Compound interest means your returns start earning returns of their own. A one-time $10,000 investment at 7% becomes roughly $76,000 in 30 years without adding a cent — and adding a steady monthly contribution multiplies the effect dramatically. The two biggest levers are time in the market and contribution consistency, not picking perfect investments.

What is a FIRE number?

Your FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early) number is the portfolio size at which a safe withdrawal rate — commonly 4% — covers your annual spending forever. Spend $40,000 a year and your FIRE number is about $1,000,000. Lowering annual spending is the fastest way to shrink the target.

Evaluating a rental property

Three numbers tell you most of the story: monthly cash flow (rent minus all costs and mortgage), cap rate (net operating income ÷ purchase price), and cash-on-cash return (annual cash flow ÷ cash invested). Many investors look for positive cash flow from day one and a cash-on-cash return above 6–8%.

Avalanche vs. snowball: which debt payoff method wins?

The avalanche method targets the highest-APR debt first and always minimizes total interest paid. The snowball method targets the smallest balance first — mathematically slightly costlier, but the quick wins keep many people motivated to finish. The debt calculator above shows the exact dollar difference for your debts, so you can decide if the motivation is worth the price.

How long will my retirement savings last?

It depends on three numbers: your withdrawal rate, your return, and inflation. Withdrawing 4% of the starting balance with inflation raises typically survives 30+ years; at 6%+ the odds shorten quickly. The drawdown calculator above simulates it month by month with inflation-adjusted withdrawals, showing the exact age your money runs out.

Is dividend investing really passive income?

Dividend portfolios pay you in cash on a schedule, making them one of the most genuinely passive income streams available. Reinvesting dividends (DRIP) during the growth years and switching to cash payouts later is the classic playbook — the calculator above models both modes.

Frequently asked questions

Quick answers to the most common money-math questions.

What return should I assume for stock investments?

The S&P 500 has historically returned about 10% per year before inflation, or roughly 7% after inflation. Using 6–7% is a common conservative default for long-term planning. Past performance never guarantees future results.

Is the 4% rule still safe for early retirement?

The 4% rule comes from the Trinity Study of 30-year retirements. For retirements of 40–50 years, many planners suggest 3.25–3.75% instead. Try both in the FIRE calculator — a 0.5% change moves the target by hundreds of thousands.

Should I pay off debt or invest first?

A common rule of thumb: pay off any debt with an interest rate above ~7% before investing beyond an employer match, since that's a guaranteed "return" comparable to stocks. Low-rate mortgages are usually kept while investing.

How much house cash flow counts as a good rental?

Many investors want at least $100–$200 per month per unit in positive cash flow plus a cash-on-cash return above 8%. The rental calculator shows both instantly.

Does dividend reinvestment (DRIP) really matter?

Enormously. Reinvested dividends buy shares that pay their own dividends — over decades this compounding typically accounts for a large share of total stock market returns. Toggle DRIP in the dividend calculator to see the gap.

Are these calculators accurate?

They use standard financial formulas (compound interest, annuity payments, amortization) computed monthly. Real-world results differ due to taxes, fees and variable returns, so treat outputs as planning estimates, not predictions.
